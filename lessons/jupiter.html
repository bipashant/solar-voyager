Jupiter and its moons

What did Galileo see that changed everything?

On 7 January 1610, Galileo Galilei pointed a homemade telescope at Jupiter and saw three small “stars” in a neat line beside it. The next night they had moved. Within a week he had found a fourth, and worked out that they weren’t stars at all. They were moons, going round Jupiter. That was a problem for anyone who believed everything in the heavens went round the Earth.

1Four dots in a line

Jupiter is the biggest planet by a mile. You could fit all the other planets inside it and still have room to spare. It’s a giant ball of hydrogen and helium with no solid surface, wrapped in stripy clouds of ammonia ice.

It has at least 95 moons, but four are big enough to see with ordinary binoculars. In order from Jupiter they are Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, and together they’re called the Galilean moons. Ganymede is the largest moon in the Solar System, bigger than the planet Mercury.

Why do they move back and forth instead of round and round? Because we see their orbits edge-on. Imagine looking at a spinning record from the side: you’d see a line, with points sliding along it and back. Here are the same moons from above and from Earth, side by side.

Why it was such a big deal

In 1610 the official view, backed by the Church and most universities, was that the Earth sat still at the centre of everything. Galileo had found four objects that plainly went round something else. If Jupiter could have its own moons, then the Earth wasn’t the centre of every motion in the sky, and the idea that Earth and the planets go round the Sun suddenly looked a lot more reasonable.

2Shadows on the clouds

Jupiter spins faster than any other planet. A whole day there lasts 9 hours and 56 minutes, which is fast enough to make the planet visibly squashed: it’s about 7% wider at the equator than pole to pole.

Its moons orbit almost exactly in line with Jupiter’s equator and with the direction of sunlight, so as they pass between Jupiter and the Sun, their shadows fall on the clouds. With a decent telescope you can watch these little black dots crawl across the planet.

A moon rhythm

Io goes round Jupiter in 1.77 days, Europa in 3.55 and Ganymede in 7.15. Each one takes almost exactly twice as long as the one inside it, a 1 : 2 : 4 rhythm. Their regular tugs on each other keep the pattern locked in, and they squeeze Io so hard that it’s the most volcanic place in the Solar System.

Try this yourself

Ordinary 10×50 binoculars will show Jupiter’s moons if you hold them very steady (lean on a wall or a car roof). Sketch the dots on one night, then again the next. You’ll see them move, just as Galileo did. Apps such as Stellarium will tell you which dot is which.

★What you learned

  1. Jupiter is the biggest planet: a gas giant with no solid surface and a day of under 10 hours.
  2. Its four big moons, from the inside out, are Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto.
  3. We see their orbits edge-on, so from Earth they look like dots sliding back and forth in a line.
  4. In 1610 they showed Galileo that not everything in the sky goes round the Earth.
  5. When a moon passes between Jupiter and the Sun, its shadow falls on the clouds. Under that shadow, the Sun is eclipsed.

A myth worth binning

“The Great Red Spot is a solid feature on Jupiter’s surface.” Jupiter has no surface to put it on. The spot is a storm, a gigantic swirl of clouds wider than the Earth, that has been raging for at least 190 years. It has been slowly shrinking for the last century, and nobody knows for sure how long it will last.
