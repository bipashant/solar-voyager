Phases of the Moon

Why does the Moon change shape?

It doesn’t. The Moon is a ball of rock 3,475 km across and it stays exactly that shape all month. What changes is how much of its lit half we can see from here — and that depends entirely on where the Moon has got to in its lap around us. Once you’ve seen it from outside, you can never unsee it.

1Half of it is always lit

Start with the same fact that began lesson 1: the Sun lights up exactly half of any ball. Earth, yes — and the Moon too. At every single moment, half the Moon is in blazing sunlight and half is in darkness.

So the Moon never actually becomes a crescent. It is always a fully lit half-ball. The only thing that changes is which part of that lit half is pointing at us.

Let’s watch a full month. This scene sits near Earth and keeps the Moon in frame while the clock runs at one day every second — so the whole 29.5-day cycle takes about half a minute.

The words

Waxing means growing, waning means shrinking. A crescent is less than half lit; a gibbous is more than half lit (from a Latin word meaning humpbacked). So the order is: new → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full → waning gibbous → last quarter → waning crescent → new.

“First quarter” looks like a half — confusingly. It’s called that because the Moon is a quarter of the way round its orbit, not because a quarter is lit.

2The same month, from above

The view from Earth tells you what happens. The view from above tells you why. Here’s the Earth–Moon system seen from above the orbit, with the Sun off to one side.

Keep your eye on two things at once: which side of the Moon is lit (always the Sun side — it never changes), and where the Moon has got to in its lap. When the Moon is on the far side from the Sun we see its whole lit face: full moon. When it’s on the near side, the lit half is pointing away from us and we see almost nothing: new moon.

Drag the Moon round the dial above and watch the two views update together. Try to land exactly on each named phase.

A myth worth binning

“The phases are Earth’s shadow falling on the Moon.” They aren’t — and here’s the proof: at full moon, Earth is directly between the Sun and the Moon, which is precisely when Earth’s shadow could reach it, and yet the Moon is at its brightest. Earth’s shadow does occasionally hit the Moon, but that’s a lunar eclipse, it lasts a couple of hours, and it only happens a few times a decade. Phases happen every month, slowly, with no shadow involved at all.

3The face that never turns away

Here’s a second mystery, and it has a lovely answer. The Moon always shows Earth the same face. Humans never saw the far side at all until a Soviet spacecraft photographed it in 1959.

It isn’t because the Moon doesn’t rotate. It’s because the Moon rotates at exactly the rate it orbits: one turn per lap. Earth’s gravity has gradually dragged it into that lock over billions of years. Astronomers call it tidal locking, and it’s common — most big moons in the Solar System do it.

Use the Jump to · Far side button in the scene above, then spin the view. Everything you recognise — the dark “seas”, the face in the Moon — is on one hemisphere. The other is a battered, crater-saturated highland almost nobody has ever looked at with their own eyes.

★What you learned

  1. The Sun always lights exactly half the Moon. The Moon never changes shape.
  2. Phases are how much of that lit half we can see from Earth, which depends on where the Moon is in its orbit.
  3. New moon: the Moon is roughly between us and the Sun, lit side facing away. Full moon: the Moon is opposite the Sun, lit side facing us.
  4. The full cycle takes about 29.5 days — which is where the word “month” comes from.
  5. Phases are not Earth’s shadow. That’s a lunar eclipse, which is rare and quick.
  6. The Moon is tidally locked: it turns once per orbit, so the same face always points at us.
