Our galaxy and beyond

Where are we in the universe?

Until about a hundred years ago, most astronomers thought the Milky Way was the whole universe. The fuzzy spirals seen through telescopes were assumed to be clouds of gas nearby. Then, in 1924, Edwin Hubble measured the distance to one of them, Andromeda, and found it was far outside our galaxy. It was a whole galaxy of its own. We now think there are hundreds of billions of them, maybe more. This lesson zooms out, one step at a time, to find our place in all that.

1Home galaxy

A galaxy is a huge collection of stars, gas and dust, all held together by gravity. Ours is called the Milky Way. It’s a flat spinning disc with spiral arms, about 100,000 light-years across, with a bulge of old stars in the middle and a black hole of 4 million Suns at its very centre.

One light-year is the distance light travels in a year: about 9.5 trillion kilometres. Light could go round the Earth seven times in a second, and it would still take 100,000 years to cross the Milky Way.

The band across the sky

On a really dark night, away from streetlights, you can see a faint, patchy river of light from one horizon to the other. That’s the Milky Way, seen from the inside. We live in the disc, so when we look along it we’re looking through billions of distant stars, too faint and crowded to see one by one. Look across the disc instead and there are far fewer stars in the way.

2The neighbours, and further

The Milky Way has company. Dozens of small galaxies orbit it, and together with Andromeda and Triangulum we make up a little gang called the Local Group. Beyond that, galaxies stretch away in every direction, as far as the best telescopes can see.

There’s a catch hiding in those distances. Light takes time to travel, so the further away something is, the older the light we see from it. Slide outwards and find out how old.

Seeing the past

The light from Andromeda that reaches you tonight left it about 2.5 million years ago. You’re not seeing Andromeda as it is now. You’re seeing it as it was when our ancestors were first chipping stone tools. There is no way, even in principle, to see it as it is today. Space telescopes like James Webb use this on purpose: by looking at the faintest, most distant galaxies, they see the universe as it was more than 13 billion years ago, when galaxies were just forming.

Try this yourself

From the northern hemisphere in autumn, find the W shape of Cassiopeia, then look below it towards the constellation Andromeda with binoculars on a dark night. The faint oval smudge is the Andromeda Galaxy. Every photon from it hitting your eye has been travelling for 2.5 million years, and has just ended its trip in your eyeball.

★What you learned

  1. The Milky Way is our galaxy: a spiral disc of 100 to 400 billion stars, about 100,000 light-years across.
  2. The Sun is about 26,700 light-years from the centre, and takes about 230 million years to go round it once.
  3. The band of light across a dark sky is the Milky Way’s disc, seen from inside.
  4. Andromeda, 2.5 million light-years away, is the nearest big galaxy, and it will probably merge with ours a few billion years from now.
  5. Light takes time to travel, so the further away we look, the further back in time we see.

A myth worth binning

“When Andromeda hits the Milky Way, stars will smash into each other.” Almost none will. Stars are so far apart compared with their size that the two galaxies will pass through each other like two swarms of gnats. Gravity will tangle them together over a billion years or so, and they’ll settle into one big galaxy. The Sun will very likely come through it fine.
