Stars through the year

Why do we see different stars in winter and summer?

If you live in the northern half of the world, Orion is the constellation of cold nights: three bright stars in a row, high in the south on December evenings. Go out at the same time in July and he’s gone. He hasn’t moved, and neither have the other stars, at least not noticeably in a human lifetime. What changes over the year is where the Earth is, and which way its night side happens to be facing.

1Your midnight window

Stars are always there, day and night. In the daytime the sky is so bright with scattered sunlight that we can’t see them. So at any moment, the stars you can see are the ones in the direction away from the Sun, out of Earth’s night side.

As Earth travels round the Sun, that night side sweeps round to face a different part of the sky. Over a year it points every way in turn, a bit like a lighthouse turning very slowly.

This scene uses a neat trick. It looks at Earth from the Sun’s direction. Stars are so far away that the ones behind Earth here are the same ones you’d see from Earth looking straight out at midnight.

Four minutes a night

Earth moves about 1° along its orbit each day, so the stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night. That adds up to two hours a month, and a full 24 hours over a year. It’s why astronomers measure a sidereal day, one turn of Earth compared with the stars, which is 23 hours 56 minutes.

2The Sun’s path through the stars

Now flip it round. Instead of looking out of the night side, look towards the Sun and imagine the sky were dark enough to see the stars behind it. Over a year the Sun appears to move right round the sky against those stars, along a line called the ecliptic. The constellations it passes through are the zodiac.

Why horoscopes are a month out

Star sign dates were set about 2,000 years ago, when the Sun really was in front of Aries in late March. Since then Earth’s axis has slowly wobbled, like a spinning top, shifting the calendar against the stars by almost a month. The wobble takes 26,000 years to go round once. On top of that, astronomers’ official boundaries (agreed in the 1920s) give the zodiac 13 constellations, not 12. The extra one is Ophiuchus, and the Sun spends about 18 days in it every year, from late November to mid December.

Try this yourself

Pick a bright star you can see from your window soon after dark, and note the time it lines up with a chimney or a tree. Check again a week later at the same spot. It will line up about half an hour earlier. In a few months, it’ll have disappeared into the evening twilight altogether.

★What you learned

  1. The stars are always there. We only see the ones on the night side of Earth, facing away from the Sun.
  2. As Earth orbits the Sun, its night side faces a different part of the sky, so the constellations change with the seasons.
  3. The Sun appears to travel once round the sky each year along the ecliptic. The constellations on that path are the zodiac.
  4. Stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night, because Earth moves along its orbit.
  5. The Sun actually passes through 13 constellations, including Ophiuchus, and the dates are about a month off from horoscopes.

A myth worth binning

“The stars in a constellation are a group, close together in space.” Almost never. The stars of Orion lie anywhere from about 250 to about 2,000 light-years away. They just happen to line up from where we stand. Seen from a planet round another star, our constellations would be scrambled beyond recognition.
