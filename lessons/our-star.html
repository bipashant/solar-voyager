Our star

What is the Sun, and how big is it really?

The Sun is a star. It sounds like something you learn once and forget, but think about what it means. Every one of the thousands of stars you can see on a dark night is a sun. Some are smaller than ours and some are far bigger. Ours only looks special because it’s close, about 150 million kilometres away. The next nearest star is 270,000 times further off.

1A star, close up

The Sun is a ball of hot gas, mostly hydrogen and helium, 1.39 million kilometres across. It has no solid surface anywhere. What looks like a surface is the layer where the gas stops being see-through and starts to glow, a bit like the edge of a cloud seen from a plane.

Deep in the middle the pressure is crushing and the temperature is around 15 million °C. In there, hydrogen is squeezed into helium. That’s called nuclear fusion, and it releases an enormous amount of energy. Every second the Sun turns about 4 million tonnes of itself into light and heat. It has been doing this for 4.6 billion years and has fuel for roughly another 5 billion.

Never look straight at the Sun

Not with sunglasses, not through binoculars or a telescope, not even for a second. It can damage your eyes for good. Everything on this page is safe because it’s on a screen. If you want to look at the real Sun, use proper eclipse glasses or make a pinhole projector out of a cereal box.

Let’s go and have a look. This scene starts at Earth, then takes you most of the way to the Sun and speeds up the clock.

The Sun is white

From space, sunlight is white. It looks yellow from the ground, and orange or red near sunset, because our air scatters some of the blue light out of the beam. That scattered blue light is the reason the sky is blue.

So how big is it?

You could line up 109 Earths across the Sun’s face. If the Sun were hollow, about 1.3 million Earths would fit inside. It also holds 99.86% of all the mass in the Solar System. Every planet, moon, asteroid and comet put together makes up the other 0.14%.

And yet it’s a fairly ordinary star. Betelgeuse, the red star in Orion’s shoulder, is so big that if it took the Sun’s place it would swallow Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars.

2The Sun from other worlds

The further you get from the Sun, the smaller it looks, the same way a streetlight shrinks as you walk away from it. This next scene keeps the zoom exactly the same and moves you from planet to planet, so you can compare.

It isn’t only the size that drops. The light gets weaker much faster than the Sun shrinks, and that matters a lot if you’re a planet trying to stay warm, or a spacecraft running on solar panels.

The inverse square rule

Sunlight spreads out in every direction. Go twice as far and the same light is spread over four times the area, so each square metre gets a quarter as much. Three times as far, a ninth. In general: brightness falls with the square of the distance.

Neptune is 30 times further from the Sun than we are, so it gets 1/900 of our sunlight. That’s still a few hundred times brighter than a full Moon, but it isn’t going to warm anything up.

Try this yourself

In a dark room, hold a torch about 30 cm from a wall and draw round the patch of light. Now step back to 60 cm. The patch is twice as wide and twice as tall, so it covers four times the area, and it looks clearly dimmer. That’s the inverse square rule on your wallpaper.

★What you learned

  1. The Sun is a star. It looks bigger and brighter than the others only because it’s so much closer.
  2. It’s a ball of hot gas, 109 times wider than Earth, powered by nuclear fusion in its core.
  3. The Sun spins. Its equator goes round about once every 25 days, its poles more slowly.
  4. From further out the Sun looks smaller. From Neptune it’s just a very bright star.
  5. Sunlight weakens with the square of the distance: twice as far, a quarter of the light.
Star
A huge ball of gas that makes its own light by nuclear fusion.
Nuclear fusion
Light atoms squeezed together into heavier ones, releasing energy. In the Sun, hydrogen becomes helium.
Inverse square
A quantity that falls with the square of the distance, like sunlight or gravity.
A myth worth binning

“The Sun is a giant fire.” Fire needs oxygen and fuel to burn, and a fire the size of the Sun would have burned out in a few thousand years. The Sun shines because of fusion in its core, which is a different process altogether and, kilo for kilo, millions of times more powerful than burning anything.
