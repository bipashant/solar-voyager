How eclipses happen

How can the Moon hide the Sun?

The Sun is 400 times wider than the Moon. It is also, by an astonishing coincidence, about 400 times farther away — so from Earth the two discs look almost exactly the same size. Line them up and the Moon blots the Sun out completely. In this lesson you’ll watch that line-up happen from four places at once: from space, from the ground, from the Moon’s point of view, and from the far side of Earth’s own shadow.

1A dot of darkness, racing

On 8 April 2024, the Moon passed exactly between Earth and the Sun. Its shadow fell on Mexico, swept north-east across the United States from Texas to Maine, and left Earth over the Atlantic — all in about three hours.

Let’s watch it happen from where the Moon was standing. Press play: the clock runs at ten minutes every second, so three hours of eclipse take about twenty seconds.

Did you notice?

The dark dot — the umbra — is small. On 8 April 2024 it was never wider than about 200 km. Everyone inside it saw a total eclipse. Everyone in the huge, faint shadow around it, the penumbra, saw only a partial eclipse: the Sun with a bite out of it.

That’s why total eclipses feel so rare. The Moon’s shadow is a pencil tip drawn across a basketball.

2Standing in the shadow

Now let’s go down to the ground and look up. This next scene puts you in Dallas, Texas on the same afternoon, looking at the Sun through a telescope-narrow view — about 2.4° across, five times the width of the Sun itself.

Watch three things: the Sun becoming a crescent, the sky going dark at totality, and the corona — the Sun’s outer atmosphere, a million degrees hot and normally drowned out by the glare — appearing around the black disc.

In real life

Never look at a partly eclipsed Sun without proper eclipse glasses — not sunglasses, not a phone camera, not smoked glass. Only during the few minutes of totality, when the Moon covers the Sun completely, is it safe to look with bare eyes. The moment the first sliver of Sun returns, glasses go back on.

Why the ring of fire?

The Moon’s orbit isn’t a perfect circle. When the Moon is near its farthest point it looks a little smaller than the Sun, and it can’t quite cover it. Instead of a black disc you get a blazing ring: an annular eclipse, from the Latin annulus, a ring.

3The other kind: a blood moon

Swap the order and you get the other kind of eclipse. When Earth comes between the Sun and the Moon, Earth’s shadow falls on the Moon. That’s a lunar eclipse, and it is a completely different experience: slow, quiet, and visible to everyone on the night side of Earth at once — no narrow path, no special glasses.

Here’s the total lunar eclipse of 14 March 2025, seen from close to the Moon.

Why red, not black?

Earth’s shadow isn’t truly dark. Earth has air, and air bends sunlight around the edge of the planet into the shadow. On the way through, the air scatters away the blue light and lets the red through — exactly what makes sunsets red.

So the light falling on an eclipsed Moon is the light of every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once, bent inwards. Standing on the Moon, you’d see Earth as a black disc ringed by a thin, glowing band of copper fire.

4So why not every month?

Here’s the puzzle. The Moon goes round Earth every 29.5 days. Once each lap it passes between us and the Sun (new moon), and once it goes round the far side (full moon). If everything sat in one flat plane, we would get a solar eclipse and a lunar eclipse every single month.

We don’t. We get two to five solar eclipses a year, and most of them are partial. Why?

Because the Moon’s orbit is tilted — by about 5.1° — against the line from Earth to the Sun. Five degrees sounds tiny. But the Moon is 384,400 km away, and 5.1° at that distance is about 34,000 km: nearly three Earths. Most months the Moon sails cleanly over or under the Sun, and its shadow misses us entirely.

Watch it happen. This scene is edge-on, looking along the Earth–Sun line, jumping from one new moon to the next through the first half of 2024.

The two places where the tilted orbit crosses the flat Earth–Sun plane are called the nodes. An eclipse can only happen when a new or full moon lands near a node — and that lines up twice a year, in what astronomers call an eclipse season. A myth worth binning

“An eclipse is the Moon’s shadow falling on Earth, so it should happen at every new moon.” The first half is right; the second forgets the tilt. The Moon’s orbit is a hoop held at a slight angle, and the shadow only lands on us when the hoop’s edge crosses the line to the Sun.

★What you learned

  1. A solar eclipse happens when the Moon passes between Earth and the Sun and drops its shadow on us. A lunar eclipse happens when Earth passes between the Sun and the Moon and drops its shadow on the Moon.
  2. The dark core of a shadow is the umbra; the faint outer part is the penumbra. Total eclipse = you’re in the umbra. Partial = you’re in the penumbra.
  3. The Moon’s umbra is only a couple of hundred kilometres wide at Earth, so a total solar eclipse is seen along a narrow path. Earth’s umbra is far bigger than the Moon, so a lunar eclipse is seen by everyone facing the Moon.
  4. When the Moon is too far away to cover the Sun completely you get an annular eclipse — a ring of fire.
  5. The Moon’s orbit is tilted about 5.1°, so eclipses only happen during the two eclipse seasons each year, when a new or full moon lands near a node.
  6. An eclipsed Moon glows red because Earth’s atmosphere bends sunset-coloured light into the shadow.
Umbra
The dark inner shadow, where the Sun is hidden completely.
Penumbra
The pale outer shadow, where only part of the Sun is hidden.
Totality
The minutes when the Moon fully covers the Sun — the only time it is safe to look with bare eyes.
Annular
An eclipse where the Moon looks too small to cover the Sun, leaving a bright ring.
Node
One of the two points where the Moon’s tilted orbit crosses the Earth–Sun plane.
Eclipse season
The few weeks, twice a year, when eclipses are possible.
