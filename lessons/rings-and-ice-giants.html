Rings and sideways worlds

Why do Saturn’s rings sometimes disappear?

Out past Jupiter the planets get colder, slower and stranger. Saturn wears the most famous rings in the sky, yet every 15 years or so they seem to vanish. Uranus spins lying on its side, so each pole gets 42 years of daylight followed by 42 years of night. And Neptune has a big moon going round it the wrong way. All three oddities come down to the same thing: which way something is tilted, and where you’re looking from.

1Saturn’s disappearing rings

Saturn’s rings are not solid. They’re billions of separate chunks of water ice, from specks of dust to lumps the size of a house, each one orbiting Saturn like a tiny moon. From edge to edge the bright rings span about 280,000 km, getting on for the distance from Earth to the Moon.

Saturn is tilted by about 27°, and its rings lie around its equator, so they’re tilted too. As Saturn makes its slow 29-year trip round the Sun, that tilt stays pointing the same way in space. So from Earth we see the rings from above for a few years, then edge-on, then from below. Watch it happen at a year per second.

Saturn would float

Saturn is the least dense planet, made mostly of hydrogen and helium. On average it’s lighter than the same volume of water, so in a bath big enough to hold it (good luck finding one) it would float. Its day is just 10 and a half hours, and that fast spin makes it the most squashed planet of all.

2Planets on their sides

Earth is tilted by 23.4°, and that gives us our seasons (lesson 2 of How the Sky Works). Uranus is tilted by 98°. It doesn’t spin like a top, it rolls round the Sun like a ball. Most astronomers think something the size of a planet slammed into it long ago and knocked it over.

One Uranus year lasts 84 Earth years. For a quarter of that, one pole points almost straight at the Sun and has constant daylight. A quarter-orbit later, the Sun is over the equator and days and nights come and go every 17 hours. Then the other pole gets its turn.

Seasons on Uranus

An 84-year orbit split into four seasons gives about 21 years each. Stand at Uranus’s north pole and you’d get 42 years of daylight, with the Sun slowly spiralling round the sky, then 42 years of darkness. Voyager 2 flew past in January 1986, right at the height of southern summer, so we have close-up pictures of only half the planet.

Try this yourself

Stick a pencil through a ball to be the axis. Walk it in a circle round a lamp, keeping the pencil pointing at the same wall the whole time. Tilt it a little and you get Earth’s seasons. Tilt it right over, almost flat, and you get Uranus: one end faces the lamp, then the middle, then the other end.

★What you learned

  1. Saturn’s rings are countless chunks of ice, about 280,000 km across but mostly only around ten metres thick.
  2. The rings are tilted with Saturn, and keep pointing the same way as Saturn orbits, so every 13 to 16 years we see them edge-on and they almost vanish. The last time was March 2025.
  3. Uranus is tilted by 98°, so each pole has 42 years of daylight and then 42 years of night.
  4. Neptune’s big moon Triton orbits backwards, probably because Neptune captured it from the Kuiper belt.
  5. All four giant planets have rings. Saturn’s are just by far the brightest.

Retrograde
Moving the opposite way to almost everything else in the Solar System.
A myth worth binning

“Only Saturn has rings.” Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune all have ring systems too. Jupiter’s are thin dust, Uranus’s are dark narrow hoops discovered in 1977, and Neptune’s are faint and clumpy. Saturn’s happen to be made of bright, clean ice, which reflects a lot of sunlight, so they’re the only ones you can see through a small telescope.
