The planet race

Why do some planets have longer years?

A year is simply one lap of the Sun. On Mercury that takes 88 days; on Neptune it takes 165 of our years. Part of the answer is obvious — the outer planets have further to go. But that isn’t the whole story, because the outer planets are also moving more slowly. In this lesson you’ll watch the race, spot the pattern, and meet the rule Johannes Kepler found in 1618.

1Four planets off the line

Let’s start close to home, with the four rocky planets. Press play and the clock runs at one month every second, so Earth completes a lap in about twelve seconds.

What you just watched

Mercury laps the Sun in 88 days — it goes round more than four times for each one of Earth’s laps. Venus takes 225 days, Earth 365, Mars 687.

Mercury isn’t just closer. It is also moving faster through space: about 47 km/s, against Earth’s 30 km/s and Mars’s 24 km/s. The Sun’s gravity is much stronger close in, and a planet has to move faster to stay in orbit against it.

2Now the giants

Zoom out and the picture changes completely. Jupiter is five times further from the Sun than Earth — but its year isn’t five times longer, it’s nearly twelve times longer. Neptune is 30 times further out, and its year is 165 times longer.

This scene runs at one year every second.

Kepler’s third law

In 1618, after decades of grinding through Tycho Brahe’s observations, Johannes Kepler found the pattern. Measure the distance in astronomical units (1 AU = Earth’s distance from the Sun) and the year in Earth years, and:

T² = a³

The year squared equals the distance cubed. Test it: Jupiter sits at 5.2 AU, and 5.2³ = 141. The square root of 141 is 11.9 — and Jupiter’s year really is 11.9 Earth years. It works for every planet, every dwarf planet, every asteroid, and for moons round their planets too.

Invent a planet in the diagram above and see where it lands on the line. Every real planet sits on that same straight line, which is what a power law looks like on log scales — and that is Kepler’s law made visible.

3Lapping, and the Great Conjunction

Because the inner planets move faster, they are constantly overtaking the outer ones on the inside — the way a runner in lane one laps someone in lane eight. When two planets line up as seen from Earth, we call it a conjunction.

Jupiter takes 11.9 years per lap and Saturn 29.4. Work out how long until Jupiter catches Saturn again and you get about 19.9 years — so a Jupiter–Saturn conjunction comes round roughly every twenty years. Most are unremarkable. Occasionally the geometry is near-perfect, and you get something like December 2020, when the two appeared just 0.1° apart: a fifth of the Moon’s width, close enough to cover with a fingertip.

★What you learned

  1. A planet’s year is the time it takes to orbit the Sun once.
  2. Planets further from the Sun have longer years for two reasons: a longer path, and a slower speed along it.
  3. Orbital speed falls with distance: Mercury races at 47 km/s, Neptune crawls at 5.4 km/s.
  4. Kepler’s third law: in AU and Earth years, T² = a³. Double the distance and the year gets about 2.8 times longer.
  5. Faster inner planets repeatedly overtake slower outer ones, producing conjunctions. Jupiter catches Saturn about every 20 years.

Go further

Kepler’s law works for anything orbiting the Sun, including comets. Halley’s Comet has a year of 76 Earth years. Can you work out its average distance from the Sun? (Hint: you need the cube root of 76², which is 5776.) The answer is about 17.9 AU — further out than Uranus.
