The life of a star

Where do stars come from, and how do they end?

Stars look permanent. They were there for the dinosaurs and they’ll be there for whoever comes after us. But they aren’t forever. Every star is born in a cold cloud of gas, shines for millions or billions of years, and then dies, sometimes quietly and sometimes in an explosion you can see from across the galaxy. We can’t watch one star go through all that. We can visit stars at every stage, though, and the app has a few of the best examples.

1Star nurseries

Space between the stars isn’t quite empty. There are huge, cold, dark clouds of hydrogen gas and dust, some of them hundreds of light-years across. When a patch of cloud gets dense enough, its own gravity starts pulling it inwards. It shrinks, spins faster and heats up, and after a few hundred thousand years the centre becomes hot enough for nuclear fusion to switch on. A star is born.

Stars rarely form alone. One cloud usually makes hundreds or thousands at once, from small dim ones to a few big bright ones. The bright ones then light up the leftover gas, and that glowing cloud is what we see as a nebula.

You can see one tonight

The Orion Nebula is visible without a telescope from almost anywhere on Earth, from roughly November to March. Find the three stars of Orion’s belt, then look below them for a short line of fainter stars, his sword. The middle “star” of the sword looks a bit fuzzy. That fuzz is the nebula, and the light you’re seeing left it around the year 680.

2How stars end

A star spends most of its life steadily turning hydrogen into helium in its core, like the Sun is doing now. What happens when the hydrogen runs low depends almost entirely on one thing: how much mass the star was born with. Heavy stars squeeze their cores much harder, burn much hotter, and use up their fuel far faster.

Now let’s go and see the remains.

Made in stars

The Big Bang made almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. Nearly everything else was built later, inside stars. Sun-like stars make carbon and nitrogen. Massive stars build oxygen, silicon and iron in their cores, and their explosions scatter it all into space. Some of the heaviest elements, like gold, were mostly forged when neutron stars collided.

The carbon in your body, the oxygen you breathe and the iron in your blood were all made in stars that died before the Sun was born.

Try this yourself

On a clear winter evening, compare Betelgeuse (Orion’s top-left shoulder, orange) with Rigel (his bottom-right foot, blue-white). Their colours tell you their temperatures: Betelgeuse is a cool red supergiant near the end of its life, Rigel a hot young giant. Both are big enough that they’ll probably end as supernovae.

★What you learned

  1. Stars form when parts of cold gas clouds collapse under their own gravity until fusion starts. Glowing star-forming clouds are called nebulae.
  2. A star’s mass at birth decides almost everything: how bright, how long it lives and how it ends.
  3. Small stars live for trillions of years. The Sun gets about 10 billion. Giant stars burn out in a few million.
  4. The Sun will become a red giant, puff off its outer layers as a planetary nebula, and leave a white dwarf.
  5. Stars of more than about 8 Suns explode as supernovae, leaving neutron stars or black holes and scattering the elements we’re made of.
Nebula
A cloud of gas and dust in space. Some are where stars are born, some are what dying stars throw off.
White dwarf
The hot, Earth-sized core left behind by a Sun-like star.
Supernova
The explosion of a massive star (or a white dwarf that gains too much mass).
Neutron star
A collapsed core about 20 km across, so dense that a teaspoon would weigh billions of tonnes.
A myth worth binning

“The Sun will explode as a supernova.” It isn’t heavy enough. Only stars of roughly eight times the Sun’s mass or more go out with a bang. The Sun will swell up, shrug off its outer layers in a slow glowing puff, and spend the rest of time as a cooling white dwarf.
