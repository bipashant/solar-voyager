How far is far?

How big is the Solar System, really?

Every poster of the Solar System lies to you. It has to — if the Sun were the size of a beach ball on that poster, Neptune would be two and a half kilometres away and far too small to print. Space is mostly, overwhelmingly, space. In this lesson you’ll ride a beam of light outwards, build a model you could actually walk, and come away with a feel for the real thing.

1The fastest thing there is

Nothing travels faster than light: 299,792 km every second. It could lap Earth seven and a half times in the time it takes you to say “light”. If that’s the fastest anything can go, it makes a useful ruler — and the answers it gives are startling.

Looking out is looking back

Light takes time to arrive, so you never see anything as it is now. The Moon you see is 1.3 seconds old. The Sun is 8 minutes 20 seconds old — if it vanished this instant, you would carry on seeing it shine for another eight minutes.

Jupiter is about 43 minutes old. Neptune, around 4 hours. And the nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.2 years old: its light set off before some of the people reading this started school.

2Fly it yourself

Reading the numbers is one thing. Here’s the scene where you do it. This one gives you the full flight console: pick a destination, pick a ship speed, set the flight clock to real time, and press Engage.

Start with the Moon at the speed of light. Then try the Sun. Then try Neptune — and then switch your ship speed down to something human and look at what the trip clock says.

Three flights worth taking
  • Earth → Moon at light speed, real time. 1.3 seconds. Blink and you miss it. This is the only trip in the Solar System that feels instant.
  • Earth → Sun at light speed, real time ×100. 8 minutes 20 seconds, squeezed to five. Notice how long “instant” turns out to be.
  • Earth → Neptune at Voyager 1’s speed. Set the ship speed to Voyager 1 and look at the trip estimate before you launch. Voyager 1 launched in 1977 and is still going.

3A model you could walk

Here’s the honest way to feel the scale: shrink everything by the same amount — sizes and distances — and see where things land.

Try the basketball. The Sun is 24 cm across; Earth is a 2 mm pinhead 26 metres away, about the length of a swimming pool. Jupiter is a 24 mm marble 134 metres off. Neptune is a 9 mm pea nearly 800 metres away — put the Sun at your school gate and Neptune is most of a kilometre down the road.

And the nearest star? On that same scale, Proxima Centauri is about 6,900 kilometres away. Sun at the school gate; nearest star on another continent. That is what the space between stars is actually like.

Why spacecraft take so long

The New Horizons probe left Earth faster than anything before it — 16.26 km/s, fast enough to cross the Atlantic in six minutes. It passed the Moon in nine hours. It still needed nine and a half years to reach Pluto.

Nothing was wrong with the rocket. The Solar System is just that big.

4The long way out

One last scene. This one starts at Earth and backs away in stages — Earth, the inner planets, the giants, then the whole Solar System — so you can watch the familiar shrink to nothing.

★What you learned

  1. Light travels 299,792 km/s — the fastest anything can go.
  2. Light takes 1.3 seconds from the Moon, 8 minutes 20 seconds from the Sun, about 4 hours from Neptune, and 4.2 years from the nearest star.
  3. Because light takes time, looking out into space is looking back in time.
  4. In a true scale model, the planets are specks and the gaps are enormous. Sun as a basketball → Earth is a pinhead 26 m away.
  5. Spacecraft take years to cross the Solar System even at tens of kilometres per second, because the distances are so vast.
  6. The gaps between stars are thousands of times bigger again than the gaps between planets.

Build it for real

Take a tennis ball for the Sun (6.7 cm). Earth is then a grain of sand 7.2 metres away, Jupiter a peppercorn at 37 m, Neptune a pinhead at 217 m. Walk it out in a park or a long corridor with sticky notes, and count your paces between the planets. Nobody forgets the walk from Saturn to Uranus.
