Falling around the Sun

Why don’t the planets fall into the Sun?

The Sun pulls on the Earth all the time. It pulls hard enough to hold a whole planet on a leash 150 million kilometres long. So why haven’t we fallen in? The surprising answer is that we are falling, constantly, and so is every planet and moon. We’re just moving sideways so fast that we keep missing. Isaac Newton worked this out in the 1680s with a thought experiment about a cannon, and you get to fire it.

1Newton’s cannon

Throw a ball and it curves down to the ground. Throw it harder and it lands further away. Newton asked what would happen if you could keep throwing harder. Because the Earth is round, the ground curves away beneath a fast enough ball. At about 8 km per second the ground curves away exactly as fast as the ball drops. It falls, and falls, and never lands. That’s an orbit.

Why astronauts float

The Space Station is only about 400 km up, and gravity there is still about 90% as strong as on the ground. The astronauts float because they and the station are falling together, all the time, at the same rate. It’s the feeling you get for a split second at the top of a rollercoaster, except it never stops. NASA calls it free fall.

A dent in space

Newton described gravity as a pull. In 1915 Albert Einstein came up with a deeper picture: mass bends space and time around it, and things moving through that bent spacetime follow curved paths. A popular way to imagine it is a stretched rubber sheet with a heavy ball in the middle. Roll a marble past and it curves round the dip.

The app can draw that sheet. In this scene each world sits in a well as deep as its gravity is strong. The depth is hugely exaggerated so you can see it, but the shapes and the comparisons between worlds are real.

Closer is faster

The steeper the slope, the faster you have to go to keep from sliding in. Mercury, deep in the Sun’s well, orbits at about 47 km/s. Earth does 30 km/s, Jupiter 13 and Neptune just 5.4. This is the same pattern you met in the planet race (lesson 5 of How the Sky Works), and Newton showed it comes straight out of gravity getting weaker with distance.

2Wells within wells

Everything with mass makes its own dent. The Moon falls round Earth inside Earth’s little well, while Earth and the Moon together fall round the Sun inside its enormous one. Jupiter’s moons circle in Jupiter’s well, which is itself a small dimple on the Sun’s.

Try this yourself

Stretch an old pair of tights or a stretchy sheet tight over a bucket or between chairs. Put a heavy ball in the middle, then flick a marble sideways across the sheet. Too slow and it spirals into the middle. Just right and it goes round a few times before friction wins. Real orbits have no friction, so they go on for billions of years.

★What you learned

  1. An orbit is falling while moving sideways so fast that you keep missing the thing you’re falling towards.
  2. Near Earth’s surface that takes about 8 km/s. At 11 km/s you escape altogether.
  3. Astronauts float because they’re in free fall along with their spacecraft, not because there’s no gravity.
  4. Planets closer to the Sun have to move faster to stay in orbit.
  5. Einstein pictured gravity as mass bending spacetime. Every world sits in its own well, and wells nest inside bigger wells.
Gravity
The attraction between all things with mass. Stronger for more mass, weaker with distance.
Free fall
Moving under gravity alone. Everything falls together, so you feel weightless.
Spacetime
Space and time treated as one thing, which mass and energy can bend.
A myth worth binning

“There’s no gravity in space.” There’s gravity everywhere. It’s what holds the Moon in orbit 384,000 km away, and what keeps the Sun circling the middle of the Milky Way 26,000 light-years off. It gets weaker with distance, but it never switches off.
