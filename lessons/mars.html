Mars, up close

What is Mars like, and when can we go?

Mars is the planet we know best after our own. Robots have been driving around on it since 1997, and right now two NASA rovers, Curiosity and Perseverance, are still at work there. It’s a cold desert world about half the width of Earth, with a day only a little longer than ours, the tallest volcano we know of, and two small moons that look like potatoes. Getting there is the tricky part, and it has a lot to do with timing.

1A tour of the red planet

Mars is red because it’s rusty. The dust that covers almost everything is full of iron oxide, the same stuff that forms on an old bike left out in the rain. Winds carry that dust high into the thin air, which is why even the Martian sky has a butterscotch tint.

The air itself is mostly carbon dioxide and about a hundred times thinner than ours. You couldn’t breathe it, and you’d need a pressure suit just to stay alive. The average temperature is around −60 °C, though a summer afternoon near the equator can reach 20 °C.

Biggest of everything

Olympus Mons is a volcano about 22 km high, two and a half times the height of Everest, and so wide that if you stood on top, the edges would be over the horizon. Valles Marineris is a canyon system about 4,000 km long, which would stretch from New York to Los Angeles. Mars has no moving plates in its crust, so its volcanoes stayed in one spot for millions of years and just kept growing.

A day on Mars

A Martian day is called a sol, and it’s 24 hours and 37 minutes long. Close enough to ours that mission teams on Earth have lived on “Mars time”, starting work 37 minutes later each day. A Martian year is 687 Earth days, so you’d have a birthday about every 23 months.

2Catching a moving target

You might think the best time to go to Mars is when it’s closest. Not quite. A spacecraft can’t carry enough fuel to fly straight there under power. Instead it fires its engines for a few minutes, then coasts most of the way on a long curved path round the Sun, a transfer orbit. That path takes about seven months, and Mars keeps moving the whole time.

So you have to launch when Mars is in the right spot ahead of Earth, so that it arrives at the far end of the curve just as you do. That lineup is called a launch window, and it only comes round every 26 months.

Why 26 months?

Earth goes round the Sun in 12 months and Mars in about 23. Each month Earth gains about 14° on Mars, so it takes roughly 26 months for Earth to lap Mars and get back to the same position relative to it. Astronomers call that the synodic period. Every Mars launch window is one synodic period after the last.

Try this yourself

Find two friends. One stands still as the Sun. One walks a small circle round them (Earth), the other a bigger circle, more slowly (Mars). Try lobbing a soft ball from Earth to where Mars will be when it lands. You’ll find you have to throw well ahead of Mars. Mission planners have the same problem, with more maths.

★What you learned

  1. Mars is red because its dust is full of iron oxide: rust.
  2. It has the biggest volcano (Olympus Mons) and one of the biggest canyons (Valles Marineris) in the Solar System.
  3. A Martian day, a sol, is 24 h 37 min. A Martian year is 687 Earth days.
  4. Phobos orbits faster than Mars spins, so from the ground it rises in the west, twice a day.
  5. Spacecraft coast to Mars on a curved transfer orbit, so they launch in windows every 26 months, when Mars is the right distance ahead.

A myth worth binning

“Mars is hot, because it’s red.” Red looks warm to us, but Mars is further from the Sun than we are and gets less than half our sunlight, with hardly any air to hold the heat. A typical night is colder than Antarctica in winter. The red is just rust.
