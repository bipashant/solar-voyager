The Moon’s hidden side

Why do we only ever see one side of the Moon?

Every person who has ever looked up has seen the same face of the Moon: the same dark patches, the same bright crater near the bottom. Nobody saw the other side until a Soviet spacecraft photographed it in 1959. It isn’t that the Moon doesn’t spin. It spins at exactly the right speed to keep one face pointed at us, and there’s a good reason for that.

1One turn per lap

The Moon goes round Earth once every 27.3 days. It also turns on its own axis once every 27.3 days. Because those two numbers match exactly, the same side always faces inwards, towards us. It’s like walking round a table while always facing the cake in the middle: by the time you’ve gone round once, you’ve also turned round once, but the cake only ever sees your front.

It helps to see what would happen if the spin were different. Try it.

The far side is not the dark side

The far side gets just as much sunlight as the near side. At new moon, when we see the near side dark, the far side is in full daylight. People say “the dark side of the Moon” when they mean the side we can’t see, which is a different thing. Spacecraft have mapped all of it, and in 2019 China’s Chang’e 4 became the first to land there.

2The same face, all month

From Earth, the lighting on the Moon changes through the month, from a thin crescent to full and back, as you saw in the phases lesson. The face itself doesn’t budge. Watch the same craters sit in the same places while the sunlight sweeps across them.

How did it get locked?

Earth’s gravity pulls harder on the near side of the Moon than on the far side, which stretches the Moon slightly into a rugby-ball shape, with bulges facing towards and away from us. Billions of years ago the young Moon spun faster. Earth kept tugging on those bulges and acting like a brake, slowing the spin until the bulges pointed straight at Earth and there was nothing left to slow. That’s tidal locking, and most big moons in the Solar System are locked to their planets.

Tides work both ways

The Moon stretches Earth too. Our oceans bulge on the side facing the Moon and on the side facing away, and Earth turns underneath both bulges each day. That’s why most beaches get two high tides a day.

The same braking happens to us, just more slowly. Earth’s day gets about 2 milliseconds longer each century, and the Moon drifts about 3.8 cm further away every year. We know because Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the Moon, and observatories bounce lasers off them.

Try this yourself

Put a chair in the middle of a room to be Earth. Walk round it in a circle, always facing it. Halfway round, notice you’re facing the opposite wall from when you started: you’ve turned through half a turn. Back at the start, you’ve turned once. Now walk round facing the same wall the whole time. Whoever sits on the chair gets to see your back.

★What you learned

  1. The Moon spins once for every trip round Earth, 27.3 days each, so the same face always points at us.
  2. That match is called tidal locking. Earth’s gravity braked the young Moon’s spin until it fell into step.
  3. The far side gets as much sunlight as the near side. It’s hidden, not dark.
  4. Pluto and Charon are locked to each other, so each always shows the other the same face.
  5. The Moon raises tides on Earth. Over time they’re lengthening our day and pushing the Moon further away.

A myth worth binning

“The Moon doesn’t rotate.” If it didn’t, we’d see every side of it over the course of a month, as you found in the diagram above. Seen from the stars, it turns all the way round once a month. Seen from Earth it seems not to turn at all, which is exactly what keeps the far side hidden.
