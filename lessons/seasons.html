The seasons

Why is summer hot and winter cold?

Almost everyone’s first answer is “because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer”. It is a very good guess. It is also wrong — and you can prove it with one fact: when it’s summer in Europe it’s winter in Australia, and Earth can’t be near and far at the same time. The real answer is a 23.4° lean that never changes direction.

1Killing the distance idea

Let’s deal with the popular wrong answer first, because it’s a good one and it deserves a proper burial.

Earth’s orbit is slightly oval. We’re about 147.1 million km from the Sun in early January, and about 152.1 million km in early July — a difference of about 3%. So far so promising.

Except: Earth is closest to the Sun in January. That’s the middle of the northern winter. And when Europe is shivering, Argentina and Australia are at the beach. One planet, one distance, two opposite seasons. The distance idea is dead.

2The lean that makes everything

Earth’s axis — the line it spins around — isn’t upright compared with its path around the Sun. It leans over by 23.4°. And crucially, it keeps leaning the same way all year, like a spinning top that holds its angle. It points at the same spot in the sky (very near Polaris, the North Star) in January and in July.

That means for half the year the northern half of Earth leans towards the Sun, and for the other half it leans away. Watch it happen.

Two things change, and they both matter

1. The angle of the sunlight. When your hemisphere leans towards the Sun, the Sun climbs higher in your sky at midday, and its light hits the ground more squarely. Squarely-hit ground gets more energy per square metre.

2. The length of the day. Leaning towards the Sun also means your part of Earth spends more of each rotation inside the lit half — so the day is longer and there are more hours to warm up, and fewer to cool down.

That first point is the one people find surprising, so it’s worth playing with. Drag the Sun lower in the diagram above and watch the same beam of sunlight smear itself over a bigger and bigger patch of ground. The light isn’t weaker. It’s just spread thinner.

3Four dates that name the year

Two moments in the year are extremes, and two are perfectly balanced.

  • June solstice (around 21 June) — the North Pole leans most towards the Sun. Longest day in the north, shortest in the south.
  • December solstice (around 21 December) — the opposite. Longest day in the south.
  • March and September equinoxes — the tilt is sideways-on to the Sun, neither pole favoured. Day and night are near enough equal everywhere on Earth. Equinox literally means “equal night”.

Move through the year in the diagram above and watch two things at once: which way the blue axis leans relative to the Sun, and the day length in whichever city you pick. Try Reykjavík — the swing is enormous. Then try Nairobi, almost on the equator, where it barely changes at all.

4The Sun that won’t set

Push the idea to its limit. If you stand far enough north in June, your spot on Earth never leaves the lit half as the planet turns — so the Sun goes round and round the sky and simply never sets. That’s the midnight Sun. Six months later, the same place gets the opposite: the Sun never rises at all.

Try this yourself

Take a globe (or an orange with a pencil through it) and tilt the pencil about 23° from vertical. Walk it slowly around a lamp, keeping the pencil pointing the same direction in the room the whole way round — don’t let it swivel to follow the lamp. That single rule is the whole of this lesson.

★What you learned

  1. Earth’s axis is tilted by 23.4°, and it keeps pointing the same way in space all year round.
  2. Seasons happen because each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for half the year and away for the other half.
  3. Leaning towards the Sun means a higher Sun (light hits more squarely) and a longer day — both make it warmer.
  4. The two hemispheres always have opposite seasons, which is the proof that distance isn’t the cause. Earth is actually closest to the Sun in January.
  5. Solstices are the extremes; equinoxes are the balanced points between them.
  6. Inside the polar circles the tilt is enough to give a Sun that never sets in summer and never rises in winter.
Axial tilt
The 23.4° lean of Earth’s spin axis compared with its orbit.
Solstice
The day when one pole leans most towards the Sun — the longest or shortest day.
Equinox
A day when neither pole leans towards the Sun, so day and night are about equal everywhere.
Midnight Sun
Summer inside a polar circle, when the Sun stays above the horizon all day and night.
A myth worth binning

“It’s summer because Earth is closer to the Sun.” Earth is closest in early January — northern mid-winter. And if distance were the cause, both hemispheres would have summer at the same time. They never do.
