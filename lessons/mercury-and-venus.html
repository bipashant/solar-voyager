Mercury and Venus

Why does Venus change shape in a telescope?

Mercury and Venus are the two planets closer to the Sun than we are. You can see both without a telescope, but never in the middle of the night, and never far from the Sun. Venus is the brilliant “star” you sometimes spot low in the west after sunset. Point even a cheap telescope at it and you’ll notice something odd: it isn’t round. In 1610 that odd shape helped settle an argument that had run for 1,400 years.

1The inside lanes

Picture the planets as runners on a circular track with the Sun in the middle. Mercury and Venus have the inside lanes. Earth is in lane three. Because their lanes are inside ours, when we look at them we’re always looking roughly towards the Sun.

That’s why neither can be high in the sky at midnight, when we’re facing directly away from the Sun. Venus never gets more than about 47° from the Sun in our sky, and Mercury no more than 28°. So you catch them in twilight: in the west after sunset (an “evening star”) or in the east before dawn (a “morning star”).

The third brightest thing in the sky

After the Sun and the Moon, Venus beats everything. It’s close, it’s about the size of Earth, and its clouds reflect around three quarters of the sunlight that hits them. On a clear evening it’s bright enough to be mistaken for an aircraft landing light, and people regularly report it as a UFO.

Galileo’s crescent

For centuries most astronomers followed Ptolemy, who put Earth at the centre with everything else circling it. In his scheme Venus always sat somewhere between Earth and the Sun, so it could only ever show us a crescent or a sliver. It could never look full.

In late 1610 Galileo watched Venus night after night through his new telescope and saw it go through phases, from nearly full and small to a big thin crescent. Ptolemy’s model simply couldn’t do that. A Venus that goes round the Sun can.

The trick is that we see different amounts of Venus’s lit half depending on where it is. When it’s on the far side of the Sun we see the lit half face on, but it’s a long way off and small. When it comes round between us and the Sun, it’s close and big, but its lit side is turned mostly away from us. Try it here.

2A very long day on Mercury

Mercury is the smallest planet and the fastest. It goes round the Sun every 88 days. It also spins, but slowly: once every 59 days. Put those two motions together and you get something strange. From one sunrise to the next on Mercury takes 176 Earth days, which is two whole Mercury years.

Day versus year

A planet’s year is one trip round the Sun. Its solar day is the time from one noon to the next. On Earth the two are wildly different: 365 days to a year. On Mercury the slow spin and the fast orbit nearly cancel out, so noon comes round only once every two years.

Venus is even stranger. It spins backwards, once every 243 days, and its year is 225 days. Its day is longer than its year, and on Venus the Sun would rise in the west. If you could ever see it through the clouds.

Try this yourself

Look up when Venus is visible this month (a quick search for “Venus evening star” and your country works). Find it at dusk, and if you know anyone with binoculars or a telescope, look at its shape. Then check again a month later. You’re repeating Galileo’s experiment.

★What you learned

  1. Mercury and Venus orbit inside Earth’s orbit, so they always appear fairly close to the Sun: in the evening or morning sky, never at midnight.
  2. Venus shows phases because we see different amounts of its sunlit half as it goes round the Sun.
  3. Venus looks biggest when it’s a thin crescent, because that’s when it’s closest to us.
  4. A full set of phases is only possible if Venus goes round the Sun. Galileo saw them in 1610.
  5. Mercury’s day, sunrise to sunrise, lasts 176 Earth days: two of its 88-day years.

A myth worth binning

“Mercury is the hottest planet, because it’s closest to the Sun.” Mercury’s day side reaches about 430 °C, but with no air to hold the heat its nights drop to −170 °C. Venus is hotter, everywhere, all the time: about 465 °C, day and night, under a crushing carbon dioxide atmosphere that traps heat. That’s hot enough to melt lead.
